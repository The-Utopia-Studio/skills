Your interviews aren't going well. They're going *pleasantly*, which is what
happens when you describe the idea and then ask what people think of it.

**The mechanism.** The moment your idea is on the table, the person's job
changes. They stop reporting their own life and start evaluating your proposal —
and evaluating a stranger's proposal politely is what nearly everyone does. So
you get "sounds great", which is a fact about human manners, not about your
product. Then they don't sign up, because liking an idea and needing it are
unrelated. Your outcome is the expected result, not a surprise.

**Four fixes, in order of how much they'll change.**

1. **Delete every question that mentions your product.** Not soften — delete. If
   a question can't be asked without describing what you're building, it's a
   pitch. This will probably remove most of your script, and that's the fix.

2. **Convert future to past.** "Would you use this" → "what did you do the last
   time this came up". You can't be polite about last Tuesday; there's either a
   story or there isn't.

3. **Convert opinion to cost.** "Is this a problem for you" → "how long did that
   take, and what did it stop you doing". A number is hard to inflate to be
   nice.

4. **Write the disconfirming answer at the top of your script.** One line: what
   would you hear that means you're wrong? If you can't write it, the interview
   has no way to come back negative and the compliments are structurally
   guaranteed.

**What to listen for instead.** The strongest signal available is an existing
workaround — a spreadsheet they maintain, a rule in their mail client, a
recurring calendar block, a person they ping. Someone who built a workaround has
already paid for this problem with their own time. Someone who says "sounds
great" has paid nothing. One workaround outweighs ten enthusiastic calls.

**What won't fix it.** More interviews, or a different segment. Twenty more
conversations with this script produce twenty more compliments. The script is
the variable.
