# Interview script — supplier email triage, procurement managers

Two things you asked for I'm not going to write, and one is the reason the rest
of this works.

**"Would they want it"** is a future-tense opinion about a thing that doesn't
exist. **"What would they pay"** is stated willingness-to-pay for a hypothetical,
asked by the person selling it. Both are the weakest evidence available — and
worse than useless, because the answers arrive sounding like data. You'd come
back with "8 of 10 said they'd want it and would pay around $200/month", and it
would predict nothing.

**What is answerable right now:** procurement managers triage supplier email
today, by some method, at some cost. That's a present-tense behaviour with a
history, and it's discoverable without your tool existing. If triage is genuinely
painful, the interviews surface workarounds, time costs, and abandoned attempts —
all of which survive the product not existing. If it isn't painful, you find that
out, which is the more valuable result and the one a "would you want it" script
structurally cannot produce.

**Hypothesis:** supplier email triage is a significant, repeated time cost that
procurement managers work around manually.
**The answer that kills it:** they don't experience it as a task at all — mail
rules and a shared inbox already absorb it, and nobody has spent time on it
because there's nothing to spend time on.

---

## Opening (2 min)

- "I'm researching how procurement teams handle inbound supplier email. I'm not
  selling anything and I don't have anything to show you."
- "No right answers — I'm trying to understand how the work actually goes."
- "Can I record? Stays with me." · "Still have 30 minutes?"

## Warm-up (4 min)

- "Tell me about your role — what's a normal week?"
- "How many suppliers are you dealing with at the moment?"
- "Who else touches supplier email besides you?"

## Core: last week's inbox (16 min)

- "Think about last week. Walk me through what came in from suppliers."
- "What did you do with it — take me through it in order."
- "Which ones needed a decision from you?"
- "How did you decide which to deal with first?"
- "What did you do with the ones you didn't action?"

**The mechanics**
- "Where does supplier email land — your inbox, a shared one, somewhere else?"
- "Do you have any rules or folders set up? Show me?"
- "What happens to something that arrives while you're in meetings all day?"

**Cost — time already spent**
- "Roughly how much of last week went on this?"
- "What were you not doing while you did it?"
- "Has anything ever been missed? Walk me through what happened."
- "Have you or anyone on the team built something to help — a rule, a
  spreadsheet, a shared doc?" *(if yes: ask to see it — this is the strongest
  artefact you can leave with)*

**Money already spent**
- "Is there anything you pay for that touches this?"
- "Has the team ever looked for a tool for it? What happened?"
- "Has anyone been hired, even partly, to handle this?"

## Probing

"Tell me more." · "Why?" · "Can you give me a specific example?" · "What
happened next?"

## Wrap-up (3 min)

- "What didn't I ask that I should have?"
- "Who else handles this that I should talk to?"

**Do not describe the tool.** If asked what you're building: "I'm still working
out whether there's anything worth building — that's what these calls are for."


## On pricing

What they already spend on this job — tooling, headcount, a retainer — is
answerable and useful. Stated willingness-to-pay for something that doesn't
exist is not, and shouldn't be fed into pricing work. Take the "money already
spent" answers to `value-based-pricing`; leave the hypothetical out.
