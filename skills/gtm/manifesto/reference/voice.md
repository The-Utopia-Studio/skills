# Voice and sentence craft

Sentence-level rules extracted from the corpus. Use this when drafting or when
rewriting someone else's draft.

---

## Tense and person

**Present tense for the world. Present continuous for you. Future tense only for
consequences.**

The corpus is overwhelmingly present tense. "Superintelligence is within reach."
"The internet is an integral part of modern life." "AI is changing how products
get built." Present tense asserts. Future tense hedges, because a claim about
2030 cannot be checked today and readers know it.

Use future tense once, near the end, for the world-after beat. Not before.

**Person:**
- Companies: "we". Consistently, from the first paragraph.
- Philosophies and founder essays: "I".
- Never the company name in third person as the subject of belief sentences.
  "At Navon, Navon believes" is the single clearest marker of a document written
  by committee.
- "You" is allowed in the direct-address beat of a Philosophy and in the
  invitation. Elsewhere it turns into ad copy.

## Rhythm

The pattern that recurs across the corpus, at both paragraph and section scale:

**Short declarative. Longer developing sentence that gives the reason or the
mechanism and earns the assertion. Short declarative.**

Concretely:

> Nuclear needs scale. For seventy years it has been built as bespoke,
> one-off projects, each one negotiated separately and priced accordingly.
> That is not an industry. That is a series of favours.

Three moves: assert, explain, land. The last sentence is shorter than the first.

**Paragraph length:** one to four sentences. Long paragraphs signal explanation,
and a manifesto is not primarily explaining.

**Fragments:** allowed, rarely, and only for the naming beat or the close. Two
in a whole document is plenty. A run of fragments reads as machine-written, and
readers now notice.

**Read it aloud.** Every stumble is a real defect. This is not a metaphor, it is
the actual test.

## Openings

Five that work, from the corpus:

1. **Bare assertion.** One sentence about the world, standing alone. No
   subject-is-a-company framing. The most confident opening available, and it
   only works if the sentence is genuinely arresting.
2. **Identity then aim.** "X is a [what]. We are building [what for whom]."
   Two sentences, no wasted motion. The workhorse opening for companies.
3. **Antithesis.** Generous account of the state of the art, then "but", then
   the exception. The Named Problem archetype almost always opens here.
4. **Definition.** Name your term and define it in the first sentence.
5. **Admission.** Something against your own interest, which buys credibility
   for everything after it.

Openings that fail: a statistic from an analyst report, a rhetorical question,
"Since the dawn of", "In today's rapidly changing world", or anything that
describes the market before it describes the world.

## The naming line

Every manifesto worth remembering has one line people can repeat. It is usually
three to six words, usually a noun phrase, and it usually contains a slight
grammatical oddity that makes it stick: "File over app" is not standard English,
which is exactly why it lodges.

Rules for building one:

- Concrete nouns. Objects beat concepts.
- No adjectives if you can avoid them.
- Say it out loud to someone who does not know the company. If they ask what it
  means, that is good. If they nod politely, it is dead.
- Put it on its own line, or as a section heading, or both. Do not bury it in
  the middle of a paragraph.
- Repeat it two or three times across the document, exactly, without variation.
  Variation kills the repeat.

## Concreteness

The manifestos that hold up name physical things. Folding a shirt. Clay tablets.
Graphite. A named factory in a named town, with the year. Three hundred
milliseconds. Thirty days of training.

Abstraction is the default failure mode of this genre because abstraction is
safe. Force at least one concrete object into every section. If a section cannot
hold a concrete noun, it is probably a section about the company rather than
about the world, and it can be cut.

**Numbers:** use only real ones. One verified number does more than a paragraph
of ambition, and one wrong number destroys the document. Never round a number
upward for rhythm.

## Hedging

Delete on sight:

| Hedge | Fix |
|---|---|
| aim to, seek to, strive to, look to | just do the verb |
| help to, work to, hope to | same |
| can, could, may (about your own actions) | will, or present tense |
| one of the leading | either say the position or drop it |
| we believe that we are | we are |
| potentially, arguably, in many ways | delete, no replacement |
| a number of, various, several | the actual count, or nothing |

Genuine uncertainty is different and should be kept. "We do not know how long
this takes" is a strong sentence. "We aim to help enable" is not uncertainty, it
is cowardice with a thesaurus.

## Banned words

These are load-bearing in bad manifestos and absent from good ones:

passionate, world-class, best-in-class, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art,
revolutionary, disruptive, game-changing, leverage (as a verb), synergy,
empower, unlock (as a metaphor), seamless, robust, holistic, ecosystem (when it
means "some companies"), journey (when it means "process"), mission-critical,
next-generation, paradigm, transform (without an object), reimagine, delightful.

Also watch for the AI tells: sentence fragments used for drama in sequence,
"It's not X. It's Y." repeated as a structural tic, tricolon in every paragraph,
and the "not just A, but B" construction more than once per document.

---

## Adjectives

The corpus uses very few. When it does, they are doing real discriminating work:
"bespoke, project-based" tells you the shape of the problem. "Undetectable"
tells you what the product is.

Rule: an adjective earns its place only if removing it changes the meaning. If
removing it only lowers the temperature, remove it. Two stacked adjectives are
almost always one too many.

---

## Section headings

In a web manifesto, headings carry the argument. A reader who reads nothing but
the headings should come away with the whole thesis.

Write them as claims, not labels. "Solid foundations matter" is a claim.
"Our approach" is a label. Labels are where conviction goes to die.

Test: read your headings in sequence with nothing between them. If it reads as a
coherent short argument, the structure is sound. If it reads as a table of
contents, restructure.

---

## Closings

Four that work:

1. **Invitation.** "Join us", meant literally, with a link to open roles. The
   most common close in the corpus and the least pretentious.
2. **Return to the naming line.** Repeat it exactly, one last time, alone.
3. **Consequence.** One sentence about what becomes possible, stated flatly with
   no adjective inflation.
4. **Concession.** Accept the limit of your own project, which paradoxically
   strengthens it. Only available if you did the self-implication work.

Closings that fail: a summary of what you just said, a list of four CTAs of
different weights, "the future is bright", or a contact form.

**One call to action.** The corpus shows several companies offering three or
four buttons, and it dilutes every one of them. If the document is for
recruiting, ask for recruits. Pick the reader you actually want.

---

## Register by audience

The same content should shift register depending on who is meant to read it:

- **Recruits:** highest conviction, most cost disclosure, hardest problem
  framing. People join for difficulty, not comfort.
- **Customers:** concrete outcomes, present-tense capability, less
  civilisational sweep.
- **Investors:** the sequence and the mechanism. The Staged Plan spine, even
  inside another archetype.
- **Regulators and institutions:** the Principles Charter grammar, explicit
  commitments, and the constraints you accept.
- **Peers and the field:** the teaching move. Name the problem better than they
  can and they will cite you.

If the user wants one document for all five, tell them what it costs: the
document will be less sharp for each. Usually the right answer is to write for
recruits and let everyone else read over their shoulder.
