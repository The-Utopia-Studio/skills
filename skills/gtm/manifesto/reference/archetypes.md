# The seven archetypes

Derived from taking apart the collection at manifestos.tech. Pick one
deliberately. Mixing two is usually how a manifesto turns into an about page.

Each entry gives: what it is for, the spine in order, the word count that fits,
the failure mode, and where to go and read a real one.

---

## 1. The Single Claim

**For:** One overwhelming conviction, an organisation whose structure is the
argument, a moment where explaining would weaken you.

**Spine**
1. A standalone assertion about the world, one sentence, no qualifier.
2. What we are, named. Often a fragment: the name of the company as a sentence.
3. Why this structure guarantees the focus. This is where the cost goes.
4. Where we are and who we want.
5. Now is the time. Join us.

**Length:** 150 to 350 words. Whitespace is doing work. If a paragraph runs past
three sentences, you are writing a different archetype.

**Why it works:** the reader assumes anyone this brief has already had the long
argument internally and won it. Brevity reads as certainty. It only works if
every sentence could be a headline.

**Failure mode:** brevity without conviction reads as an unfinished page. If you
cannot make each sentence quotable, write 600 words instead.

**Read:** Safe Superintelligence (`ssi.inc`), which is roughly 230 words and
spends a third of them on why its funding structure removes short-term pressure.

## 2. The Named Problem

**For:** You can articulate a bottleneck better than anyone else in your field.
The best default for a technical company.

**Spine**
1. **Antithesis opening.** Everything has advanced, and here is a list of the
   advances, generously described. Then: but.
2. **The exception.** The thing that has stubbornly not moved.
3. **The name.** Your coinage for it. This is the whole document's payload.
4. **Why it is hard.** Show you understand it deeply. Cite the paradox, the
   constraint, the thing everyone underestimates.
5. **Why now.** What changed that makes it tractable this decade.
6. **Our bet.** What you are building, specifically.
7. **Join / collaborate / reach out.**

**Length:** 800 to 3,000 words. The technical case earns the length.

**Why it works:** the reader learns something. A manifesto that teaches gets
forwarded. The naming step is what people remember and repeat, and repetition is
distribution.

**Failure mode:** the name is generic ("the data problem") or the antithesis
opening is stacked so high that the piece reads as a survey. Spend at most two
sentences being generous about the state of the art.

**Read:** Physical Intelligence's π0 post, which opens by listing a decade of AI
wins, pivots on the gap between screen intelligence and physical intelligence,
and anchors it on Moravec's paradox before showing any technique.

## 3. The Principles Charter

**For:** An institution, a coalition, a standard, or a company whose behaviour
needs to be predictable to outsiders. Written to be cited by other people later.

**Spine**
1. **Preamble.** Why this document exists and what it governs.
2. **Numbered principles.** Stable grammar throughout. Pick one form and hold
   it:
   - Descriptive: "The internet is a global public resource."
   - Prescriptive: "Individual privacy must not be optional."
   - Committal: "We will not compete where competing would harm the mission."
   Mixing the three is fine only if the sequence is deliberate: descriptive
   first, prescriptive next, committal last.
3. **Commitments.** What the author will do, distinct from what they believe.
   Keep these separate and clearly labelled. Beliefs are free; commitments cost.
4. **The ask.** What the reader must do for this to hold.

**Length:** 1,000 to 2,000 words. Ten principles is the ceiling. Four is often
better.

**Why it works:** it survives leadership changes and gets quoted in arguments
years later, including against its author. That is the point.

**Failure mode:** principles that no one could oppose. Run every clause through:
would a serious organisation write the opposite? If not, cut it. "We value our
users" is not a principle.

**Read:** the Mozilla Manifesto, which separates ten principles from a small set
of explicit commitments and keeps the grammar of each block consistent.

## 4. The Philosophy

**For:** An idea larger than your company, which your product happens to serve.
Written so that people who never buy from you still adopt the idea.

**Spine**
1. **Definition.** Name the philosophy in the first sentence and define it
   immediately. No throat clearing.
2. **Historical proof.** Reach far back. The corpus reaches for clay tablets,
   coal and oil, the printing press. Distance makes the claim feel structural
   rather than commercial.
3. **The present failure.** What today's default gets wrong, described as a
   condition rather than a brand.
4. **Direct address.** Speak to the people who could act on this: makers, tool
   builders, institutions.
5. **Self-implication.** Apply the philosophy to yourself, including where it
   hurts. Concede your own obsolescence if that is where the logic goes.

**Length:** 400 to 700 words. Aphorism density matters more than coverage.

**Why it works:** it becomes a phrase people use without attribution, which is
the strongest distribution a company can have.

**Failure mode:** the philosophy conveniently describes exactly your feature
set. If it does, it is positioning wearing a philosophy costume. The test is
step 5: if the idea cannot cost you anything, it is not an idea.

**Read:** "File over app" by Steph Ango, roughly 520 words, which argues from
hieroglyphs to plain text and ends by accepting that Obsidian will die.

## 5. The Staged Plan

**For:** A strategy that looks absurd or elitist at step one and only makes
sense as a sequence. Also good when you are asking people to fund step one.

**Spine**
1. **Admit the obvious objection first.** The thing critics will say. Say it
   yourself, plainly, in the opening.
2. **The numbered sequence.** Usually three to five steps. Each step must fund
   or unlock the next. Make the dependency explicit, that is the whole argument.
3. **The economics.** Real numbers. Why the expensive first step is necessary
   rather than indulgent.
4. **The endpoint.** What the sequence adds up to, stated once, without
   embellishment.
5. **A wink or a signature.** This archetype tolerates personality better than
   any other.

**Length:** 700 to 1,500 words.

**Why it works:** it converts a weakness into a plan. Readers who came to
object leave holding a roadmap.

**Failure mode:** steps that are parallel rather than sequential. If step three
could happen without step two, you have a list of products, not a plan.

**Read:** Tesla's 2006 master plan, which opens by conceding the first car is a
low-volume expensive sports car and spends the piece explaining why that is
step one rather than the strategy.

## 6. The Personal Testament

**For:** A founder whose own history is the reason the company exists. Values
documents that need to be felt rather than complied with.

**Spine**
1. **An admission.** Something slightly against interest. Discomfort with the
   genre itself works well and buys enormous credibility.
2. **One anchoring story.** A scene with real detail: places, ages, objects,
   what was on the radio. Specificity is the entire mechanism.
3. **Values named as behaviours,** each one returning to the story. Use verb
   phrases, not nouns. "Assume you don't know" rather than "Humility".
4. **Numbered sub-points under each value,** which keep it usable rather than
   ornamental.
5. **An emotional close,** not an imperative one.

**Voice:** first person singular. "I" throughout, with "we" reserved for the few
moments where the personal becomes collective. This is the only archetype where
"I" beats "we".

**Length:** 1,500 to 6,500 words. This is the one form where long is right,
because the story needs room.

**Why it works:** nobody can copy your childhood. Every other archetype is
imitable.

**Failure mode:** a story with no cost or no failure in it. If the anecdote
flatters the author, it reads as a founder profile.

**Read:** The Browser Company's values essay, which frames five values around a
family road trip and opens by admitting corporate values make the author
uncomfortable.

## 7. The Historical Inevitability

**For:** A technology whose moment has arrived, especially in energy, hardware,
manufacturing, and anything capital-intensive where you need the reader to feel
that the money is following a pattern rather than taking a flyer.

**Spine**
1. **The sequence.** Two or three prior eras, each named by its enabling
   technology, each with what it built. Coal, then oil, then this.
2. **The stall.** Why the next era has not started. Usually structural:
   regulation, one-off projects, capital formation, supply chain.
3. **The unlock.** What changed. This must be recent and real.
4. **The scale claim.** Not a better unit, a different order of magnitude, and
   the mechanism that gets you there.
5. **Physical specifics.** Named sites, materials, dates, capacities. This
   archetype dies without them.
6. **Multiple entry points:** join, buy, partner.

**Length:** 800 to 1,500 words.

**Why it works:** it borrows the certainty of history. The reader is not being
asked to believe in you, only that the pattern continues.

**Failure mode:** the historical sweep is not paid off by present-tense
specifics. Two paragraphs of civilisation followed by "we are excited to
announce" is the most common way this form collapses.

**Read:** Valar Atomics and Hadrian. Both run a civilisational frame and then
immediately ground it in named facilities, materials and timelines.

## Choosing under uncertainty

If the user has strong material but you cannot tell which shape fits, ask which
of these they would rather a reader say afterwards:

- "They are completely certain." → Single Claim
- "I understand the problem now." → Named Problem
- "I know how they will behave." → Principles Charter
- "I have changed how I think." → Philosophy
- "That is actually going to work." → Staged Plan
- "I want to work with that person." → Personal Testament
- "This was always going to happen." → Historical Inevitability
