# Audit checklist

Run this against a draft before delivering, and use it when asked to critique an
existing manifesto. Report failures rather than silently fixing them: some are
the author's call, not yours.

Score honestly. A document that passes the four gates and half the rest is
better than one that passes everything except a gate.

---

## The four gates

Fail any of these and the document is not a manifesto yet. Say so directly.

**1. Contestability.**
Could a competent, well-funded competitor publish the opposite claim and mean
it? Take the load-bearing sentence, invert it, and read the inverse aloud. If
the inverse is absurd, you have written a description of reality, not a
position.

**2. The cost.**
Does the document name something the author is giving up? Revenue, speed,
optionality, a market, a customer segment, the permanence of their own product.
If there is no sacrifice anywhere in the text, the reader has no reason to
believe the conviction is real.

**3. Falsifiability of the build.**
Is what they are making concrete enough that they could visibly fail at it?
"Advancing the state of the art" is not falsifiable. "Three reactors on one site
by 2027" is.

**4. Datedness.**
Could this have been published five years ago, unchanged? If yes, it is a
truism. Something in the document must depend on what is true now.

## Structure

- [ ] The load-bearing sentence appears in the first two paragraphs.
- [ ] There is a naming line, three to six words, repeated exactly two or three
      times.
- [ ] Section headings read in sequence as a coherent argument, not a table of
      contents.
- [ ] The world-state beat comes before any description of the company.
- [ ] The adversary is a condition, not a named competitor.
- [ ] The world-after beat is specific: it names something a person can do that
      they cannot do today.
- [ ] There is exactly one call to action, and it is an invitation rather than a
      form.
- [ ] The archetype is consistent. It has not started as a Named Problem and
      drifted into a values list halfway down.

## Sentences

- [ ] Present tense for the world throughout. Future tense used once at most.
- [ ] First person plural for a company, singular for a philosophy or founder
      essay. Never third-person self-reference in belief sentences.
- [ ] No hedges: aim to, seek to, strive to, help to, hope to, look to,
      potentially, arguably.
- [ ] No banned words: passionate, world-class, cutting-edge, revolutionary,
      leverage, synergy, empower, seamless, robust, holistic, next-generation,
      reimagine, disruptive.
- [ ] No adjective that could be removed without changing meaning.
- [ ] No paragraph longer than four sentences.
- [ ] At most two sentence fragments in the whole document.
- [ ] "Not just X, but Y" appears at most once.
- [ ] Every section contains at least one concrete noun: an object, a place, a
      material, a date.
- [ ] Every number is real and verifiable. Nothing rounded up for rhythm.

## Content

- [ ] Every claim about the company is something they could start acting on this
      quarter.
- [ ] No value statement that a reasonable organisation would oppose.
- [ ] Beliefs and commitments are visibly distinct, if both are present.
- [ ] Any historical framing is paid off by present-tense specifics within two
      paragraphs.
- [ ] Nothing invented: no traction, customers, capacity, or partnerships that
      do not exist. This is the most damaging failure available, because the
      genre invites it.
- [ ] The document says something the author would be uncomfortable having a
      rival read. If not, it is safe, and safe is the same as forgettable.

## Length

- [ ] Body copy sits in the band the archetype calls for.
- [ ] Nothing survives that would read equally well on a different company's
      page.
- [ ] The second example was cut when the first one landed.

## Final pass

- [ ] Read aloud, start to finish, without stopping. Every stumble marked and
      fixed.
- [ ] The founders have read it aloud too. A manifesto nobody can say in their
      own voice will not be said.

---

## Reporting a critique

When auditing someone else's document, lead with the gates, then the two or
three highest-leverage structural fixes, then a short list of line edits. Do not
produce an exhaustive line-by-line pass on a document that fails a gate: fix the
position first, the sentences second.

State plainly which failures you can fix and which need a decision from the
author. The cost gate almost always needs a decision, because naming a sacrifice
is a commercial choice and not a writing one.
