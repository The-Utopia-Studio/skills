# Deal Sheet

## What it is

One page. The document that gets forwarded.

An investor who likes you does not forward your 18-slide deck to a colleague with a paragraph of explanation. They forward one page and write "thoughts?". If that page does not exist, they write the summary themselves, in their words, with their errors — or they don't forward it at all.

The deal sheet is also the discipline document. If the offer does not fit on one page, you do not yet know what you are selling.

## The eight blocks

In this order, on one A4 page. Roughly 350–500 words of actual prose; the rest is figures.

1. **The one-liner** (1 sentence). What the company is, in words a generalist partner can repeat. No category invention, no "AI-powered platform for". If it needs a comma splice and three adjectives, it is not ready.
2. **The round** (a table, 5–7 rows). Instrument, amount, pre-money, minimum cheque, amount already committed, close date, entity and jurisdiction. State the instrument precisely — "SAFE, post-money, no discount, MFN" not "equity round".
3. **Traction** (3–4 stat tiles). The strongest verifiable facts, with an as-of date. Contracted revenue beats pipeline. Signed beats LOI. A named customer beats a count.
4. **Why now** (2–3 sentences). The change in the world that makes this the moment. Must be external to you — a regulation, a cost curve, a supply shift. "We just launched" is not a why-now.
5. **Business model** (2–3 sentences or a 3-row table). What you charge, who pays, and the unit. One number for gross margin.
6. **Use of funds** (3–5 bullets with percentages or amounts). What the money buys and, for each line, what it proves. "$2.1M — production line two, unlocks the second offtake contract." Never "team, product, marketing".
7. **The milestone this round buys** (1 sentence). What is true when the money is spent, that is not true now. This is the sentence the partner repeats internally.
8. **Contact and the room** (1 line). Name, email, link to the reading room.

Optional ninth block if you have it: **the independent evidence** — a third-party report, an audit, a named pilot result. One line. This is worth more page space than any adjective.

## Judgment

**The number that goes first.** Lead traction with the figure that is hardest to argue with, even when a different figure is larger. $1.4M contracted beats a $40M pipeline; a partner discounts pipeline by 90% and resents being asked not to.

**State the price.** Founders leave valuation off to "keep it open". Investors read that as either inexperience or a bad number. If you genuinely have not priced it, say "priced with the lead" — that is a position; silence is not.

**Use of funds is where credibility is won.** Generic allocations signal you have not planned. Each line tied to a milestone signals you have. This block changes more minds than the one-liner.

**No adjectives on your own work.** Remove "revolutionary", "world-class", "cutting-edge", "best-in-class". Every one of them is read as a substitute for a fact, correctly.

**Fit it honestly.** If it overflows, cut the narrative, never the numbers. Two pages is not a deal sheet; it is a short memo that will not be forwarded.

## What good looks like

Score a draft out of 6. Below 5, it is not ready to send.

| # | Test |
|---|------|
| 1 | A partner can restate the business in one sentence after 30 seconds |
| 2 | Instrument, amount and pre-money are all explicitly stated |
| 3 | Every traction figure is verifiable and carries an as-of date |
| 4 | Every use-of-funds line names what it proves |
| 5 | The why-now is external to the company |
| 6 | Zero self-congratulatory adjectives |

## Gotchas

- **Do not call it a teaser unless it is genuinely anonymous.** A teaser hides the company name for a sale process. A deal sheet names you. Using the wrong word invites the wrong expectations.
- **"Committed" has a meaning.** It means signed or wired. Verbal interest is not committed, and a partner who discovers the difference stops believing the other figures.
- **The as-of date.** Traction figures without a date go stale invisibly and get quoted back at you in month four.
- **One page means one page in print.** Check the PDF, not the browser. See `investor-doc-system`.
- **Don't restate the deck's table of contents.** The deal sheet is the offer, not a summary of your narrative.
- **Update it before every send.** It is the most-forwarded and most-copied document you own, so it is the one where a stale number does the most damage. Run the fact check (`investor-fact-ledger`).

## Worked example — use of funds, before and after

Before:

> Use of funds: engineering (40%), go-to-market (30%), operations (20%), working capital (10%).

After:

> - **$2.4M — production line two.** Takes capacity from 400 to 1,100 units/month and unlocks the signed $3.1M offtake, which is capacity-contingent today.
> - **$1.6M — two field engineers and a service depot.** Cuts installation lead time from 9 weeks to 3, the single largest cause of slipped revenue in 2026.
> - **$1.2M — certification for the EU market.** 11-month process; starting now puts first EU revenue in Q4 2028.
> - **$0.8M — 18 months of buffer** past the milestone above, so the next round is raised on proof rather than on runway.

Same money. The second version tells a partner you have a plan, and gives them four sentences to use in IC.
