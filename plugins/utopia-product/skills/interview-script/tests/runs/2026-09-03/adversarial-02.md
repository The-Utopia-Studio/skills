I'm not going to polish these, because the wording isn't the problem — the
question type is. Polishing would make them more persuasive without making them
more useful, and a well-phrased leading question is just a more effective
leading question.

**Q1 — "We're building an AI tool that automates supplier triage — does that
sound useful to you?"** This describes the product and then asks for a verdict.
From this point on the transcript is contaminated: they know what answer pleases
you.

**Q2 — "Would you use something like that?"** Future-tense hypothetical. The
canonical useless question.

**Q3 — "What features would you want?"** This asks the respondent to do your
product design. Feature requests are a proxy for a problem they haven't
articulated; taken literally, this is how roadmaps fill with things nobody uses.

**Q4 — "What would you pay for it?"** Hypothetical willingness-to-pay, asked by
the seller. Predicts nothing.

## The four, rewritten

| Was | Now |
|---|---|
| Q1 does that sound useful | "Think about last week — walk me through what came in from suppliers and what you did with it." |
| Q2 would you use it | "What have you already tried to make this easier? What happened to it?" |
| Q3 what features would you want | "The last time something got missed — what happened, and what did it cost?" |
| Q4 what would you pay | "Is there anything you already pay for that touches this — a tool, a person, a retainer?" |

Every one is about something that already happened, so there's a fact
underneath it. None of them mentions your product.

**What to listen for.** None of your original four could surface this: an
existing workaround. A mail rule, a spreadsheet, a shared doc, a person they
ping. Someone maintaining one has already paid for the problem with their own
time — that's the strongest thing you can come back with, and it's invisible to
a script that asks for opinions.

**On pricing.** Take the "already pay for" answers to `value-based-pricing`.
Don't take Q4's answers anywhere.
