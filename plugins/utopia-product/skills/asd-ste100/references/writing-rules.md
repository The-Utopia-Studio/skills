# The 65 writing rules — ASD-STE100 Issue 9

Part 1 of the standard, in nine sections, plus the eight general recommendations.
Rule text is quoted from Issue 9 (January 2025); the notes under each rule are the
operative detail you need to apply it. The published standard is the authority:
https://asd-ste100.org (free download). © ASD.

**Reading order when you are rewriting:** words (1) → verbs (3) → sentences (4) →
procedural (5) or descriptive (6) → safety (7) → punctuation and word count (8).

---

## Section 1 — Words (1.1–1.14)

**Which words can you use?**

- **1.1 — Use words that are: approved in the dictionary, technical nouns, or technical verbs.**
  Those three sources are the whole permitted vocabulary. Nothing else.
- **1.2 — Use approved words from the dictionary only as the specified part of speech.**
  `CHECK` is approved as a noun, not a verb → "DO A CHECK OF THE VALVE", not "check the valve".
  Same for `TEST (n)`, `DAMAGE (n)`, `COVER (TN)`, `OIL (n)`.
- **1.3 — Use approved words only with their approved meanings.**
  `FOLLOW` is approved only in the sense "come after", so "follow the instructions" is wrong →
  "OBEY THE INSTRUCTIONS". Check the meaning column, not just the headword.
- **1.4 — Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives.**
  The dictionary lists the permitted forms (base, -s, past, past participle). Comparative and
  superlative adjective forms exist only where the dictionary gives them.

**Technical nouns**

- **1.5 — You can use words that you can include in a technical noun category.** (22 categories, below.)
- **1.6 — Use a word that is not approved in the dictionary only when it is a technical noun or part of a technical noun.**
- **1.7 — Do not use words that are technical nouns as verbs.** "Oil the bearing" → "LUBRICATE THE BEARING WITH OIL".
- **1.8 — Use technical nouns that are approved in your company, industry, or subject field.** Company glossary or terminology database wins.
- **1.9 — When you must select a technical noun, use one which is short and easy to understand.**
- **1.10 — Do not use regional, slang, or jargon words as technical nouns.**
- **1.11 — Do not use different technical nouns for the same item.** One item, one name, everywhere.

**Technical verbs**

- **1.12 — You can use verbs that you can include in a technical verb category.** (4 categories, below.)
- **1.13 — Do not use technical verbs as nouns.** "Do a drill of the hole" → "DRILL THE HOLE".

**Spelling**

- **1.14 — Use American English spelling unless other official directives tell you differently.**
  Merriam-Webster is the reference. "Color", not "colour"; "liter", not "litre".

### The 22 technical noun categories (Rule 1.5)

A noun you cannot find in the dictionary is permitted only if it fits one of these:

1. **Official parts information** — design items from parts catalogs or drawings: bolt, cable, clip, contact, engine, filter, oil seal, propeller, switch, transceiver
2. **Vehicles or machines, and locations on them** — aircraft, airframe, cabin, cargo hold, cockpit, deck, fuselage, galley, ship, submarine, wing root
3. **Tools and support equipment, their parts, and locations on them** — access ladder, chock, drill, gauge, jack, rigging pin, test rig, torque wrench, trestle
4. **Materials, consumables, and unwanted material** — adhesives, lubricants, solvents, debris, swarf
5. **Facilities, infrastructure, and logistic procedures** — hangar, runway, warehouse, supply chain terms
6. **Systems, components and circuits, their functions, configurations, and parts**
7. **Mathematical, scientific, engineering terms, and formulas**
8. **Navigation and geographic terms**
9. **Numbers, units of measurement and time (and their symbols)**
10. **Quoted text** — placards, labels, display text quoted verbatim
11. **Professional roles, individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities**
12. **Parts of the body**
13. **Common personal effects, food, and beverages**
14. **Medical terms**
15. **Official documents, parts of documentation, standards, and guidelines**
16. **Environmental and operational conditions**
17. **Colors**
18. **Damage terms** — corrosion, crack, dent, scratch
19. **Computer science, information and communication technology**
20. **Civil and military operations**
21. **Law and regulations**
22. **Animals, plants, and other life forms**

### The 4 technical verb categories (Rule 1.12)

A verb you cannot find in the dictionary is permitted only if it fits one of these:

1. **Manufacturing processes** — remove material (drill, grind, mill, ream, unsolder); add material
   (flame, insulate, remetal, retread); attach material (braze, crimp, solder, weld); change strength
   or structure (anneal, cure, freeze, heat-treat, magnetize, normalize); change surface finish
   (buff, burnish, passivate, plate, polish); change shape (blend, cast, extrude, spin, stamp)
2. **Computer processes and applications** — input/output (click, enter, press, print, swipe, tap, type);
   user interface (clear, close, copy, cut, delete, drag, enable, erase, filter, highlight, maximize,
   minimize, navigate, open, paste, save, scroll, sort, zoom in); system operations (abort, boot,
   debug, download, format, install, load, process, reboot)
3. **Instructions and information for applicable subject fields** — e.g. navigation, medicine, trades
4. **Law and regulations**

Technical verbs obey all of section 3 (verb forms and voice) like any other verb.

## Section 2 — Multi-word nouns (2.1–2.2)

- **2.1 — Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words.**
  "Horizontal cylinder pivot bearing" (4) and "stainless steel corrosion protection strips" (5) are
  too long. Break them up with a preposition: "the pivot bearing of the horizontal cylinder".
- **2.2 — When a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full.** Then make it clear by
  (a) giving a shorter form you then use consistently, or (b) hyphenating the words that act as one
  unit: "the inward-outward valve".

Long noun strings are the single biggest source of ambiguity in technical text: the reader cannot
tell which word modifies which.

## Section 3 — Verbs (3.1–3.7)

- **3.1 — Use only the verb forms that are given in the dictionary.**
- **3.2 — Use only these verb forms and tenses:** infinitive, imperative (command), simple present,
  simple past, simple future, past participle **as an adjective**. Nothing else — no present perfect,
  no past perfect, no continuous/progressive tenses, no conditionals.
- **3.3 — Use the past participle form as an adjective.** "The removed part", "the installed unit".
- **3.4 — Do not use auxiliary verbs to make complex verb constructions.**
  "has been removed" → "WAS REMOVED" or, better, "REMOVE THE …". `BE`, `CAN`, `MUST`, `DO` are
  approved, but not stacked into perfect or continuous constructions.
- **3.5 — Use the "-ing" form of a verb only as a technical noun or as a modifier in a technical noun.**
  "the landing gear", "the operating pressure" — permitted. "while removing the panel" — not permitted.
- **3.6 — Use the active voice.** In **descriptive** writing you can use the passive voice only when the
  agent is unknown. In procedures the passive is never permitted — an instruction is a command.
  "The valve must be closed by the operator" → "CLOSE THE VALVE."
- **3.7 — Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun or other parts of speech.**
  "Do an installation of the seal" → "INSTALL THE SEAL." Put the action in the verb.

## Section 4 — Sentences (4.1–4.5)

- **4.1 — Write short and clear sentences.** Length limits are per writing type: 20 words in
  procedures (5.1), 25 words in descriptive text (6.3).
- **4.2 — Do not omit words or use contractions to make your sentences shorter.**
  No "don't", no telegraphic style. Keep articles, keep "that".
- **4.3 — Use a vertical list for complex texts.** When a sentence carries several conditions, items,
  or parallel actions, break it into a vertical list. Each list item is introduced by a lead-in
  sentence ending with a colon.
- **4.4 — Use connecting words and connecting phrases to connect sentences that contain related topics.**
  `AND`, `BUT`, `THEN`, `AS A RESULT`, `AT THE SAME TIME`. Not "however" (→ `BUT`), not
  "therefore" (→ `THUS` / `AS A RESULT`).
- **4.5 — When applicable, use an article (the, a, an) or a demonstrative adjective (this, these) before
  a noun or a multi-word noun.** Dropping articles is a common habit in technical writing and is not
  permitted in STE.

## Section 5 — Procedural writing (5.1–5.5)

- **5.1 — Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence.**
- **5.2 — Write only one instruction in each sentence** unless two or more actions occur at the same time.
- **5.3 — Write instructions in the imperative (command) form.** "REMOVE THE COVER." Not "the cover
  should be removed", not "you will remove the cover".
- **5.4 — When there is a condition that the reader must know about first, start the instruction with a
  descriptive statement.** Then divide that statement from the command with a comma:
  "If the pressure is more than 800 kPa, close the valve."
- **5.5 — Write notes only to give information, not instructions.** A note never contains a command. If
  it tells the reader to do something, it is a work step, not a note.

## Section 6 — Descriptive writing (6.1–6.6)

Descriptive writing gives information, not instructions — so the imperative form is not permitted.
It covers descriptions of items and systems, general information text (reports, brochures, papers),
and notes inside procedures.

- **6.1 — Give information gradually.** One subject per sentence. Do not stack several facts into one
  sentence and expect the reader to unpack them.
- **6.2 — Use key words and key phrases to give your text a logical structure.**
- **6.3 — Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence.**
- **6.4 — Use paragraphs to show related information.**
- **6.5 — Make sure that each paragraph has only one topic.**
- **6.6 — Make sure that no paragraph has more than six sentences.**

## Section 7 — Safety instructions (7.1–7.3)

Definitions used by aerospace and defense technical publications:

- A **warning** tells the reader that there is a risk of injury or death.
- A **caution** tells the reader that there is a risk of damage to objects.

Other industries may use "danger", "attention", or "notice", or graphical symbols. Whatever words
you use, the content must obey 7.1–7.3 (see ISO 45001, ANSI Z535, ISO 3864).

- **7.1 — Use an applicable word (for example, "warning" or "caution") to identify the level of risk.**
- **7.2 — Start a safety instruction with a clear and accurate command or condition.** The action the
  reader must take comes first — not the explanation.
- **7.3 — Give an explanation to show the risk or possible result.** The explanation comes second.

> WARNING: DO NOT TOUCH THE EXHAUST DUCT. THE SURFACE IS HOT AND CAN BURN YOU.

Command first, then consequence. A safety instruction that opens with the hazard description buries
the action the reader needs.

## Section 8 — Punctuation and word count (8.1–8.7)

- **8.1 — You can use all standard English punctuation marks but not the semicolon (;).**
  A semicolon joins two sentences that should be two sentences.
- **8.2 — Use hyphens (-) to connect words that are directly related.**
- **8.3 — You can use parentheses:** to reference illustrations or text; to include item letters or
  numbers; to identify work steps; for abbreviations; to give singular and plural at the same time;
  to explain a word or part of a sentence; to include an alternative.

**Word count** (this is how you check 5.1 / 6.3):

- **8.4 — In a vertical list, a colon (:) has the same effect on word count as a period** and shows the
  end of a sentence. So the lead-in and each list item are counted separately.
- **8.5 — When you put text in parentheses, it counts as one word in that sentence.**
- **8.6 — Count each of these elements as one word:** numbers; numbers together with units of
  measurement ("800 kPa" = 1); abbreviations; alphanumeric identifiers; quoted text; titles,
  headings, placards, and labels; proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations, and
  geopolitical entities.
- **8.7 — Hyphenated words count as one word.**

## Section 9 — Writing practices (9.1–9.4)

- **9.1 — Use a different sentence construction to write a sentence when a word-for-word replacement is
  not sufficient.** The dictionary's alternatives are a starting point, not a mechanical substitution
  table. If no alternative carries the meaning, restructure the sentence.
- **9.2 — Use each approved word correctly.** Approved does not mean "usable anywhere" — part of
  speech and approved meaning still bind.
- **9.3 — When you use two words together, do not make phrasal verbs.** "Set up", "carry out", "put
  out" are not permitted unless the dictionary approves that exact combination.
- **9.4 — When you select terminology or wording, always use a consistent style.** The same thing is
  called the same name and described the same way across the whole document set.

## General recommendations (GR-1 — GR-8)

These are not rules. They prevent the errors writers make most often.

- **GR-1 — The conjunction "that".** Use it as much as possible: "Make sure **that** the valve is open."
  It marks where the main clause ends, and many languages cannot omit the equivalent word, so it
  helps translation.
- **GR-2 — The preposition "with".** Approved in three senses (association, help/sharing, means/
  instrument) and a frequent source of ambiguity. "Install the panel with the green fasteners" has
  three readings. Reread every sentence containing "with". When you rewrite to avoid it, keep the
  primary action verb: "SEAL THE OPENING WITH TOOL TS9867", not "use tool TS9867 to seal the opening".
- **GR-3 — How to use pronouns.** Only dictionary-approved pronouns ("she"/"he" are not approved). If a
  pronoun could refer to more than one noun, repeat the noun instead.
- **GR-4 — The pronoun "this".** Make sure the reader knows what "this" points at; if it could point at
  more than one thing, restate the context.
- **GR-5 — False friends.** A word that looks like one in your native language may mean something else
  in English ("disposition" is not an instruction).
- **GR-6 — Latin abbreviations.** Do not use "e.g.", "i.e.", "etc." Write "for example", "that is", and
  either list the remaining items or drop the open-ended tail.
- **GR-7 — Inclusive language.** STE complies with gender-neutral language requirements. Gender-specific
  pronouns are not permitted; "man" and "woman" are not permitted unless the context requires them
  (for example, a medical text).
- **GR-8 — Possessive form.** Permitted, but only when you are sure it is correct — the Saxon genitive
  has no equivalent in many languages. If in doubt, rewrite without it.
